About This Model

How Beyond the Arc DL turns a forecast into a bet

← Back to today's predictions

What this is

Beyond the Arc DL is a college basketball forecasting and betting system. Two neural networks produce a score forecast for every game between two Division I teams from pregame information only. A separate decision layer then compares that forecast with the market and decides whether a bet has value, on which side, and how much to stake. The forecast never sees the betting line. The line enters exactly once, as an input to the decision, so the model is always betting against the market rather than blending with it.

The workflow

The forecast

  • Inputs are strictly pregame. Season-to-date team statistics, roster context, rest, site and the point in the season, built so that nothing from the game itself or from later games can leak in. Before a team has played, its statistical profile starts from a preseason prior built from its previous season, the prior-season lines of its returning roster and the preseason ratings; its own results take over from its first game. A team in its first Division I season has none of that history, so its games are forecast only once it has played, and are shown without a stake until it has a run of its own results.
  • An IC hedge model adjusts each team's season-to-date profile toward what the matchup implies, with bounded moves. It predicts the inputs the game will be played with, not the score.
  • An ensemble of ten thousand members is generated around those hedged profiles, after a recent-tempo adjustment, within each team's recent variability. The scoring model scores every member. The ensemble's mean is the forecast on the sheet and its shares are the probabilities; there is no separate point forecast. The width of the ensemble is then matched, game by game, to how far forecasts like it have really missed, so a probability on the sheet is neither bolder nor more timid than the record supports.
  • Early in the season a team's own box scores are too thin to carry a forecast. In a team's first few games the margin leans mostly on a simple ratings formula built from the same pregame inputs, and the full model takes over as the games accumulate.
  • The total gets its own pace forecast. How many possessions a game will have is forecast separately, from team ratings, box scores and play-by-play, and blended with the main model's pace before the total is scored. The margin does not use it.
  • Calibration and pregame adjustments correct the score level through the season and account for who sat a team's last game, for altitude and for postseason back-to-backs.

The decision

The rules follow one measurement: how the forecast's error compares with the market's on the same games. On margins the forecast starts the season well behind the market and has nearly caught it by midseason. On totals it never catches it. So spreads carry the bets, they are phased in as teams play games, and the model's own totals are shown without a stake.

  • Spreads. One rule. Our margin has to disagree with the spread by enough points to matter, and games with very large spreads are left alone. Nothing is staked until both teams have played enough games for the forecast to have caught up with the market; the stake is reduced through the next stretch of games and reaches full size after that. Every forecast is also run a second time on an independent set of team ratings. The second run never makes or changes a pick; when the two runs disagree materially about the margin, the bet is still made, at half its stake. In testing, the picks that did not depend on whose ratings the model was given won clearly more often than the ones that did.
  • Moneylines. No moneyline rule of its own survived testing. The forecast picks the same winner as the market in the great majority of games, and its disagreements on price alone did not pay. A moneyline is staked only on an underdog the spread rule already backs, at a short enough price and a reduced size. No moneyline signal is shown at all until both teams have played enough games.
  • Totals. The model's total is on the sheet for every game and is not staked. Across the seasons tested, its disagreements with the market's total did not win often enough to cover the price, and no second opinion changed that. One totals bet is staked, and it does not come from the model: the under at neutral sites, a market pattern that has held over many seasons, at a small fixed size under its own daily cap. A total is either bet or passed; there is no middle tier.
  • Tracked, not staked. Several more signals are recorded on every sheet and graded with no money on them: the under in games where the market's total tends to run high; a short-priced underdog the forecast has winning outright; the total from a widely used public ratings projection when it sits far from the market's; and whether the forecast's two runs agree on a spread pick. Any of them gets a stake only if its live record earns it.
  • Sizing. Fractional Kelly on the probability the staking rule settles on, with per-bet, per-game and daily caps. Games the market prices as near coin flips are staked at a reduced size. A confidence score from 0 to 100 accompanies every staked bet: it is where that bet ranks among past bets of the same kind, so a high score marks the bets that have historically been the strongest. It reads zero until a bet can be staked.
  • Parlay of the day. One five-leg parlay a day, built from the sheet's spread picks at a small fixed stake. It is there for interest, sits outside the bankroll and the staking rules, and is graded every morning like everything else. A parlay multiplies whatever edge its legs have, in either direction, so early in the season, before the spread picks have shown an edge, it is expected to lose. The sheet says so beside it.

How it is kept honest

Two things are structural and hold everywhere. The forecast never sees the betting line — it cannot quietly copy the market and then present the agreement as skill. And every signal on the sheet, staked or not, is graded against the final score and the book’s own number; the season record page is generated from that ledger, not written by hand. One sheet a day, the one published at a fixed time in the morning, is the bet of record: the ledger grades that sheet, not whichever came last.

The record page also puts the forecast's error beside the market's, and beside a widely used public ratings projection, on the same finished games. That table is the plainest check on the forecast itself, separate from any bet.

What an audit changed

In November 2026 an audit found that the earlier backtests were flattered by hindsight. Some team inputs in the historical files were end-of-season values, so a forecast for a November game had been made with information that did not exist until March. The backtests were rebuilt from forecasts that use only what was known on the day, and every betting rule was tested again on those.

Most of the earlier rules did not survive. The agreement-of-two-models rule on spreads, the totals profiles and the stand-alone moneyline rule all lost their edge once the hindsight was removed, and they were retired. What is described above is what was left.

What the record page is not

The three past seasons shown there are a backtest, not a track record: each season replayed through today's rules. Four things keep them from being a clean out-of-sample test:

  • The scoring network drew its validation sample at random from every season, so the forecasts behind those seasons are partly in sample. The matchup model is cleaner: the version behind each backtest season was not trained on that season's games.
  • The betting rules were chosen by looking at these seasons. Which markets to bet, how large a disagreement has to be, when in the season to start — all of it was selected on the same data it is scored on.
  • Two of the three seasons are priced off a soft book and only the most recent off the sharper one. The soft seasons look better for that reason alone; the sharp one is the fairer guide to what live play will face.
  • A backtest assumes every bet was available at the price shown. Real books and real limits do not guarantee that.

So the honest reading is that the edge is plausible across three seasons and established on none of them yet. The next genuinely clean sample is live play, and it starts from zero. Because nothing is staked until teams have played enough games, the live staked record does not begin until well into December.

Changing a rule

A change has to beat the current rule on every season available, and hold up on the sharp-book season rather than being carried by the soft ones — a rule that wins only in aggregate is not kept. Once live results exist they outrank any backtest, and a rule the live season contradicts goes, whatever the backtest said.

Specific thresholds, coefficients, feature definitions and model files are not published.
